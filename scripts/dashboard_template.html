      How it works

      Three steps, drawn live from the home in the estimator above. Change it and these change with it.

      1. It starts from a typical listing

        HomeCast learned from 3,600 real Gurgaon listings; the dots above are 400 of them, picked at random. Before it knows anything about your home, its guess is the typical listing, —.

      2. The first tree asks about your home

          500 small trees nudge the price

          Each tree asks a few yes-or-no questions and moves the price a little, fixing part of what the trees before it missed. The line is your price after each one, from — to —.

        1. It gives a fair range, not one magic number

          On listings it never saw, 8 in 10 asked between — and — its estimate when the society was named, and about 3 in 4 when it wasn't. For your home that is —.

        Where the errors land

        Out-of-fold log error, log(predicted) − log(actual), . The marked lines are the 10th and 90th percentiles that set the fair range.

        What the model leans on

        Gradient-boosting feature importance, which sums to 1 across the model's features.

        Where it is weakest

        The error is not evenly spread. Sorted into price fifths, the mean signed log error runs +0.110, +0.022, +0.008, −0.016, −0.088, trending down across the range. The model overprices the cheapest homes and underprices the most expensive, the classic pull toward the middle of a log-target regression. The cheapest fifth is also the least accurate in percentage terms, at 24.8% MAPE against the overall 18.4% (society given) and 19.9% (society not given). Treat the estimate as weakest under about ₹1 crore and above about ₹10 crore.

        FlatHouse

        Flats and houses barely share a price scale

        The cleaned dataset holds 2,799 flats and 801 houses. Median flat: ₹1.39 Cr. Median house: ₹4.25 Cr, about 3.1× more. That gap is why is_house earns 4.8% of the model's importance despite being a single 0/1 flag.

        Two location signals, not one

        Median prices across the 45 well-covered sectors run from ₹0.41 Cr to ₹12.5 Cr, a 30× spread end to end. The sector's ₹/sq.ft. carries 5.5% of the model's importance and the housing society's own ₹/sq.ft., a much finer-grained location signal, carries 14.8%, right behind area's 57.6%. Which building you're in matters almost as much as which sector.

        Old stock looks priciest until you split it

        Rank ₹/sq.ft. by age across all listings and Old Property tops the table. Split by type and it inverts: 58% of old listings are houses, and houses sit at ₹20,000/sq.ft. against ₹8,367 for flats.

        Within flats alone, the oldest stock is ₹8,886/sq.ft., below under-construction at ₹9,502. The age effect in the aggregate was property type in disguise.

        The rule of thumb gets two-thirds of the way

        Pricing by sector-median ₹/sq.ft. × area explains 65.3% of the variance (R²) on its own. A flat city-wide ₹/sq.ft. explains only 29.9%. The model reaches 82.5% with a society matched, 81.1% without one. The remaining lift comes from size, type, fit-out and community, not from knowing the sector better.

        How "what moved the price" is worked out

        Every node of every tree carries the average price signal of the training listings that reached it. Walking your home down a tree, each question moves that running value from one node to the next, and the move is credited to the detail the question asked about. Added up over all 500 trees, the credits plus the starting point give exactly the estimate, so the breakdown is the model's own arithmetic rather than a separate approximation. The page groups the details the way a buyer would (location, society, size, type, rooms, fit-out, age) and applies them in that order, so each rupee figure is the change at that step. When no society is named, the society feature holds the sector's own rate, so its share counts as location.

        From raw listings to this page

        Six stages, each one a command in the homecast CLI and each one covered by tests.

        1. Collect

          3,803 raw Gurgaon listings: price, area, bedrooms, bathrooms, furnishing, age, and a per-listing amenity score, keyed to a sector.

        2. Clean

          −126 exact duplicates, −17 listings with no price, −60 percentile outliers on ₹/sq.ft. and area. 3,600 rows survive, with the row count logged at every step.

        3. Engineer

          Thirteen features, including sector and society target encodings, both re-learned inside each training fold rather than once up front. Society is masked out of about half the training rows so the model degrades gracefully when a query doesn't supply one.

        4. Train

          Gradient-boosted regression on log price: n_estimators 500, max_depth 5, learning_rate 0.05, subsample 0.9, seed 7. No tuning: the defaults already beat both baselines.

        5. Evaluate

          5-fold cross-validation against sector-median ₹/sq.ft. × area and city-median ₹/sq.ft. × area, reported with and without a society supplied. The 10th to 90th percentile of the log errors becomes the fair range: −23.4% to +27.4% around the estimate.

        6. Ship

          All 500 trees export to JSON as plain arrays, and this page walks them in about ten lines of JavaScript. A test asserts that walker matches scikit-learn to 1e-9, so the page can never drift from the model.

        About the data

        These listings are asking prices, not confirmed sales: sellers set them, not buyers. The data is a single undated snapshot, likely scraped around 2023, not a live feed, so the prices reflect that market rather than today's. Government registration data would fix both; it isn't wired in yet.
