Findings

Four things the data insists on

Everything here comes out of the same 3,600 cleaned listings the model is trained on.

Two markets wearing one city's name

400 sampled listings · price (₹ crore) against built-up area (sq.ft.)

Composition

Flats and houses barely share a price scale

The cleaned dataset holds 2,799 flats and 801 houses. Median flat: ₹1.39 Cr. Median house: ₹4.25 Cr — about 3.1× more. That gap is why is_house earns 4.8% of the model's importance despite being a single 0/1 flag.

Location and community

Two location signals, not one

Median prices across the 45 well-covered sectors run from ₹0.41 Cr to ₹12.5 Cr — a 30× spread end to end. The sector's ₹/sq.ft. carries 5.5% of the model's importance and the housing society's own ₹/sq.ft. — a much finer-grained location signal — carries 14.8%, right behind area's 57.6%. Which building you're in matters almost as much as which sector.

Simpson's paradox

Old stock looks priciest until you split it

Rank ₹/sq.ft. by age across all listings and Old Property tops the table. Split by type and it inverts: 58% of old listings are houses, and houses sit at ₹20,000/sq.ft. against ₹8,367 for flats.

Within flats alone, the oldest stock is ₹8,886/sq.ft. — below under-construction at ₹9,502. The age effect in the aggregate was property type in disguise.

Baselines

The rule of thumb gets two-thirds of the way

Pricing by sector-median ₹/sq.ft. × area explains 65.3% of the variance (R²) on its own. A flat city-wide ₹/sq.ft. explains only 29.9%. The model reaches 82.5% with a society matched, 81.1% without one — the remaining lift comes from size, type, fit-out and community, not from knowing the sector better.

Where it is weakest

mean signed log error by price quintile · positive means the model asked too much

The error is not evenly spread. Sorted into price fifths, the mean signed log error runs +0.110 → +0.022 → +0.008 → −0.016 → −0.088, trending down across the range. The model overprices the cheapest homes and underprices the most expensive — the classic pull toward the middle of a log-target regression. The cheapest fifth is also the least accurate in percentage terms, at 24.8% MAPE against the overall 18.4% (society given) / 19.9% (society not given). Treat the estimate as weakest under about ₹1 crore and above about ₹10 crore.

Methodology

From raw listings to this page

Six stages, each one a command in the homecast CLI and each one covered by tests. The city is a parameter throughout, which is why the cities in the selector above are a data question, not a rewrite.

Collect

3,803 raw Gurgaon listings: price, area, bedrooms, bathrooms, furnishing, age, and a per-listing amenity score, keyed to a sector.

Clean

−126 exact duplicates, −17 listings with no price, −60 percentile outliers on ₹/sq.ft. and area. 3,600 rows survive, with the row count logged at every step.

Engineer

Thirteen features, including sector and society target encodings — both re-learned inside each training fold rather than once up front, and society is deliberately masked out of ~50% of training rows so the model degrades gracefully when a real query doesn't supply one.

Train

Gradient-boosted regression on log price. n_estimators 500 · max_depth 5 · learning_rate 0.05 · subsample 0.9 · seed 7. No tuning: the defaults already beat both baselines.

Evaluate

5-fold cross-validation against the two rules an agent would otherwise use — sector-median ₹/sq.ft. × area, and city-median ₹/sq.ft. × area — reported both with and without a society supplied at predict time, since real users mostly don't have one. The 10th–90th percentile of the log errors becomes the band: −23.4% to +27.4% around the estimate. Near-symmetric in log space; the lopsided percentages are just what exponentiating a symmetric log interval does.

Ship

All 500 trees export to JSON as plain arrays. This page walks them in about ten lines of JavaScript; a test asserts that walker matches scikit-learn to 1e-9, so the page can never drift from the model.
